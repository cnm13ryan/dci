# 1. Red-team findings

### F1. The reference set collapsed to a single easy reference, so (\tau) no longer cleanly identifies focal-specific interference

**Issue.** After Round 1, every contrast in the programme uses the single reference (C_{L3}), with arms of the form ({B,C_{L3}}) throughout §§6–9. Round 1 had already shown strong reference dependence: (\tau) ranged from approximately (+0.30) for ordinary references to (-0.57) for the semicolon reference, while the pooled estimate was approximately (+0.02). The retained reference is also near the failure floor. §5.2 reports control failure rates of only (0.004)–(0.006), and §3.2 reports zero failures for two ordinary references.

**Why it matters.** When
[
P(F_A=1\mid \text{reference arm})\approx 0,
]
the contrast
[
\tau \approx P(F_A=1\mid \text{focal arm}).
]
The reference subtraction therefore contributes almost no adjustment. The resulting estimate cannot cleanly distinguish focal-specific interference from the simpler possibility that the focal instruction is intrinsically difficult in that context. Every downstream headline quantity based on this reference, including (+0.400), (+0.950), (\Delta_{\text{boundary}}), (\Delta_{\text{placement}}), (\Psi), and (\Gamma), inherits this identification problem.

This also conflicts with the project's preregistered treatment of (\tau) as a family of reference-specific effects. The registration explicitly argues that collapsing across references discards the heterogeneity that motivated the reference family and that an inconvenient reference cannot simply be removed.

**Severity:** Critical.

**Evidence:** `DCI_exploratory_global_analysis.tex` §§2.1, 3.1, 5.2; `claude/dci_v15_preregistration_draft.md` on the six-reference family, min/max claim rule, and (\epsilon_{\text{ref}}) caliper certificate; `Section_9.2_Choosing_Your_Manipulation.md` on active and passive controls.

### F2. The canonical zero in the control context is floor-degenerate, and its zero-width interval is an artefact

**Issue.** The headline result reports bracket-background
[
\tau=0.000;[0.000,0.000]
]
with zero failures across all 20 tasks. The figure caption acknowledges that the corresponding bar required a nominal width merely to remain visible. Yet the marginal failure probabilities in the focal and reference arms are not reported.

The four-state results imply target compliance of approximately (0.975) or higher in both arms, placing both close to the failure floor. With roughly 40 observations per arm and zero observed events, the data remain compatible with a non-trivial underlying event probability. A nonparametric cluster bootstrap cannot represent this uncertainty when every sampled cluster contains zero events, so it returns a zero-width interval by construction.

**Why it matters.** The report correctly refuses to interpret this structure elsewhere. §10.1 states that (\tau=0-0=0) under `TARGET_CEILING` is not evidence that interference is absent. The same degeneracy nevertheless supplies one half of the central C4 dissociation. This creates an internal inconsistency and substantially overstates the precision of the bracket-background result.

The claimed dissociation therefore compares a floor-limited control context with a non-floor semicolon context. Before the difference can support a substantive conclusion, the control context must be evaluated at an operating point with sufficient failure-rate headroom.

**Severity:** Critical.

**Evidence:** `DCI_exploratory_global_analysis.tex` §§7.3, 7.4, 10.1; `section_8.1_reliability_notes.md`; `section_6.5_computing_precision.md`.

### F7. The headline dissociation is an interaction claim, but the interaction is never estimated

**Issue.** §7.3 reports separate (\tau) estimates for bracket and semicolon backgrounds under (b=0) and natural reasoning, then concludes that the backgrounds are statistically indistinguishable at (b=0) and that the dissociation emerges under reasoning. **Why it matters.** The relevant estimand is the change in the between-background contrast:
[
\Delta_r = \tau_{\text{semicolon},r} - \tau_{\text{bracket},r},
]
followed by
[
\Gamma = \Delta_{\text{natural}} - \Delta_{b=0}.
]
Separate confidence intervals for the constituent (\tau) values cannot establish this interaction. Overlapping intervals also do not establish equality.

C4 therefore requires a direct estimate of (\Gamma) from the paired task structure before the claim that the dissociation "emerges under reasoning" is supported.

### F9. The inferential procedure is poorly matched to the data structure and has not been calibrated

**Issue.** The programme relies almost entirely on percentile task-clustered bootstrap intervals over 20 clusters, generally with only 2–4 observations per cluster and a binary outcome. # 2. Concrete recommendations

### R1. Restore the reference family

**Change.** Treat (\tau) as a reference-indexed family rather than a single-reference scalar.

**Implementation.** Re-run the canonical no-thinking and natural-reasoning endpoints using at least four admitted references, ideally the six specified in the registration. Select them using the registered matching and caliper procedure, and require their isolated target-failure rates to lie in a usable range, such as (0.10)–(0.60). Report
[
\tau_j
]
for every admitted reference (j), together with (\min_j\tau_j), (\max_j\tau_j), and the marginal failure probabilities in both arms.

**Success criterion.** The semicolon-versus-bracket dissociation persists for (\min_j\tau_j), with every reference arm retaining at least (0.10) target-failure headroom. Otherwise, describe the phenomenon as reference-dependent heterogeneity.

**Priority:** P0.

### R2. Remove floor-degenerate cells from load-bearing claims

**Change.** Treat zero-event cells as boundary-limited measurements and move the control condition to an informative operating point.

**Implementation.** Report the two constituent arm-level failure probabilities beside every (\tau). Use an interval procedure that remains informative at the boundary. Re-run the bracket-background natural-reasoning condition at a harder setting, such as (k=5) or a six-sentence requirement, selected prospectively so that both arms have meaningful failure-rate headroom.

**Success criterion.** No load-bearing estimate has a zero-width uncertainty interval, and the bracket-background result is reproduced in a cell where both arms have at least (0.10) target-failure headroom.

**Priority:** P0.

### R3. Use the preregistered SESOI to distinguish equivalence from insufficient precision

**Change.** Adopt (\pm0.10) risk difference as the operational equivalence region and remove categorical null claims that cannot exclude effects of that magnitude.

**Implementation.** Compute and report minimum detectable effects and expected interval widths for (\tau), (\Delta), and (\Gamma) at 20 task clusters. Reanalyse (\Delta_{\text{placement}}), (\Gamma), (\Psi), the SmolLM3 contrast, and the six-order partition results using a formal equivalence procedure against (\pm0.10). Relabel failed equivalence tests as `UNDERPOWERED` or `NOT_IDENTIFIED`.

**Success criterion.** Every statement of "no effect" or "refuted" is supported by equivalence at (\pm0.10); every other null result is explicitly described as inconclusive.

**Priority:** P0.

### R4. Replicate the load-bearing results on fresh tasks

**Change.** Introduce a genuinely held-out task sample with explicit provenance.

**Implementation.** Define the target task population and sampling rule. Draw at least 40 new tasks that are disjoint from the existing 20-task bank, the 64-task calibration set, and all preregistered confirmatory topics. Pre-specify the natural-endpoint background dissociation and (\Delta_{\text{boundary}}) as the two primary replication contrasts. Also publish a provenance map connecting the existing 20 tasks to every registered split.

**Success criterion.** Both primary contrasts reproduce in sign on the new tasks, with point estimates lying within the original uncertainty intervals, and no existing exploratory task overlaps the confirmatory bank.

**Priority:** P0.

### R5. Map every exploratory claim to an actual confirmatory instrument

**Change.** Correct §12.3 and make the exploratory-to-confirmatory pathway explicit.

**Implementation.** Create a coverage table for C1–C8 showing which registered study can test each claim: V15 confirmatory, V16 pilot, or none. Record the relevant design mismatches, including model, (k), background set, reference family, mechanism scope, and transport scope. For claims with no suitable instrument, either preregister an appropriate confirmatory study or state explicitly that confirmation is not currently planned.

**Success criterion.** Every substantive claim has either a named confirmatory test or an explicit `NO CONFIRMATORY INSTRUMENT` designation.

**Priority:** P0.

### R6. Re-establish the reasoning mode claim using non-binding budgets

**Change.** Base reasoning conclusions exclusively on budget cells in which the cap rarely binds.

**Implementation.** Re-run positive budgets such as (256,384,512,768,) and (1024) with independently generated, content-addressed seeds. Admit a budget to substantive interpretation only when the cap-hit rate is at most 2% in every condition. If (b=256) remains binding, identify the smallest tested non-binding budget and restrict the claim accordingly.

**Success criterion.** Control-side repair is reproduced at one or more positive budgets with cap-hit rates (\leq2%). Otherwise, withdraw the "any thinking at all" formulation.

**Priority:** P0.

### R7. Estimate the headline interaction directly

**Change.** Make the background-by-reasoning interaction the estimand corresponding to C4.

**Implementation.** Estimate
[
\Delta_{0} = \tau_{\text{semicolon},0} - \tau_{\text{bracket},0},
]
[
\Delta_{\text{natural}} = \tau_{\text{semicolon,natural}} - \tau_{\text{bracket,natural}},

using the same resampled task indices so that pairing is preserved.

**Priority:** P0.

### R8. Define inferential families and control multiplicity within each family

**Change.** Organise the exploratory estimates into prospectively defined inferential families.

**Implementation.** Separate headline (\tau/\Delta/\Gamma), order effects, four-state outcomes, and cross-model analyses. Recompute each family using the simultaneous procedure specified in the registration, such as studentised max-(T) intervals with Holm-adjusted bootstrap (p)-values on shared resamples. Standardise the bootstrap implementation across all branches. Add an A/A experiment and a treatment-label permutation analysis to measure the empirical behaviour of the estimator under a known null.

**Success criterion.** The model-based and bootstrap headline estimates agree to within one interval half-width, and the null-calibration experiment produces a false-positive rate compatible with the nominal level.

**Priority:** P1.

**Implementation.** Add at least two additional non-boundary marks that are prospectively screened for high compliance, minimal semantic-content induction, and compatibility with the segmentation pipeline. For each condition, report diagnostics such as numeric-token frequency, output length, topic shift, and hand-labelled segmenter error.

**Success criterion.** (\Delta_{\text{boundary}}) remains positive across all admissible non-boundary controls, with no material differential segmentation error. Otherwise, state the result specifically as a semicolon-versus-degree-symbol effect.

**Priority:** P1.

### R11. Recompute mechanism and gate statistics on an intent-to-treat basis

**Change.** Define the primary mechanism estimands over all assigned rows.

**Implementation.** Recalculate splice-proxy prevalence and the anti-splice manipulation gate without conditioning on punctuation compliance. Report compliance itself as a separate treatment outcome. If a compliance-conditional estimand remains scientifically useful, accompany it with an explicit identification analysis, such as principal-stratification assumptions or partial-identification bounds.

**Success criterion.** The mechanism separation and manipulation-gate decision persist under the unconditional analysis. If they do not, revise the corresponding mechanism conclusions.

### R12. Audit replicate independence for every canonical dataset

**Implementation.** For every Transformers-era canonical run, calculate the byte-identical replicate-pair rate and within-task outcome correlation. Define a prospective threshold for unacceptable duplication, such as 5%, and regenerate every claim-bearing dataset that exceeds it.

**Success criterion.** Every dataset labelled `Canonical` in Appendix F has a measured duplication statistic, and C1–C3 depend exclusively on datasets that pass the declared threshold.

**Priority:** P1.

### R13. Apply the registered primary scoring rule consistently

**Change.** Count every outcome other than a full pass as target failure in the primary analysis.

**Implementation.** Treat unscorable, truncated, ceiling-bound, and no-final-within-budget rows as (F_A=1). Report the frequency of every row state separately by condition. Retain any alternative treatment of these rows as a labelled sensitivity analysis.

**Success criterion.** The primary analysis contains no condition-dependent exclusion, row-state rates are visible for every condition, and the primary and sensitivity estimates agree in sign for the headline contrasts.

**Priority:** P1.

### R14. Require successful manipulation uptake before interpreting cross-model transport

**Change.** Establish a prospective uptake criterion for every model-transfer experiment.

**Implementation.** Set a minimum background-compliance threshold, such as (0.70), together with a minimum target-failure headroom requirement. Adapt the wording using calibration tasks until the target model passes these gates. If satisfactory uptake cannot be achieved, classify the transport comparison as `NOT_IDENTIFIED_UPTAKE`.

### R15. Validate mechanism proxies on genuinely held-out annotations

**Change.** Freeze each structural proxy before evaluating its measurement performance.

**Implementation.** Construct a fresh stratified validation sample of at least 100 responses, balanced across relevant backgrounds and reasoning conditions, and exclude every response used during proxy development. Obtain independent human labels and report precision, recall, and inter-rater agreement by condition. Version and freeze the resulting scoring rule.

**Success criterion.** Held-out inter-rater agreement reaches approximately (\kappa\geq0.80), measurement error does not differ materially across conditions, and the mechanism conclusions remain stable when recomputed using the validated proxy.

### R16. Separate demonstrated capability from observed prioritisation

**Change.** Reserve "choice" and "arbitration" language for settings where the model has demonstrated meaningful capacity for joint compliance.

**Implementation.** Run a capability probe using interventions such as few-shot demonstrations or an in-context witness solution and estimate the attainable joint-compliance rate. Where focal compliance remains near floor, describe the empirical outcome states directly. Qualify all abstract-level conclusions by the relevant checkpoint, task bank, reference design, and constraint load.

# Shortcut-learning hypotheses for the DCI exploratory results

This section develops candidate shortcut-learning explanations for the DCI exploratory results. Every proposed rule is a hypothesis about a decision procedure the model *could* be using. None is claimed to be the mechanism responsible for the observed behaviour. The purpose is to identify simple surface-sensitive rules that can reproduce the existing observations and to distinguish them from richer explanations involving planning, arbitration, or explicit reasoning.

## Scope and conceptual transfer

The shortcut-learning framework asks whether a system succeeds by using the intended solution procedure or by relying on a feature that happens to predict the correct behaviour on the training distribution. A shortcut becomes visible when that feature is separated from the intended solution under distribution shift ([Geirhos et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07780)).

For DCI, the framework can be mapped as follows.

* **Intended solution.** Satisfy every instruction in the composed prompt simultaneously. In the present design, joint satisfiability is established by verifier-passing witness answers (DCI §7.4).
* **Reference regime.** As a working hypothesis, instruction-tuning and preference data provide many examples in which formatting constraints occur in relatively canonical forms and can often be solved independently.
* **Distribution shift.** DCI composes several countable and potentially structurally competing constraints, varies their order, and replaces one instruction with a matched reference. Composition itself therefore supplies the relevant shift.
* **Shortcut.** A generation rule that responds to prompt-surface features such as glyph identity, quantifier scope, serial position, or familiar collocations, and that succeeds in ordinary settings while failing when those features are decorrelated from the intended solution.

This framing imposes a useful evidential discipline. A result should not be attributed to affordance-sensitive planning or explicit constraint arbitration when a simpler surface-sensitive decision rule explains the same pattern. The interpretation advanced in the DCI analysis, namely that a mark capable of functioning as a sentence boundary induces an output structure that consumes the units counted by the target instruction (DCI §12.2), is therefore one candidate mechanism among several.

Several superficially plausible alternatives are already inconsistent with the exploratory evidence and can be excluded from the candidate set. Colon *emission* does not covary with amplification: production varies from 0.15 to 0.95 across conditions, while the within-condition association is −0.049. Placement freedom is null at every tested reasoning budget. Single-character atomicity cannot explain the result because the ellipsis also amplifies interference. A general rule based on "any freely placed unattached mark" is inconsistent with the degree-symbol control. Finally, the central behaviours occur under greedy decoding. At (q=3), failure reaches 1.000 and 97.5% of outputs contain one sentence. Candidate explanations therefore need to account for the model's modal completion rather than stochastic sampling variation.

## Candidate feature inventory

The following features provide a common vocabulary for the hypotheses below.

| Tag     | Candidate feature                       | Candidate decision rule                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| ------- | --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **F1**  | **Glyph-checkability**                  | Constraints are easier to execute when they reduce to a direct operation such as "emit character (c) exactly (n) times" or "avoid character (c)." Constraints requiring word counting, cross-sentence comparison, or syntactic-relation checking lack an equally direct surface operation and may receive weaker behavioural control. |
| **F2**  | **Boundary-unit mismatch**              | Boundary-capable marks such as `;`, `:`, `…`, and `.` can affect the model's effective segmentation of the response. If that segmentation differs from the verifier's sentence definition, the model may generate a structure that appears internally adequate while failing the external sentence-count verifier.                    |
| **F3**  | **Right-context continuation prior**    | Marks such as `;` and `:` strongly predict continuation within an existing sentence. After emitting such a mark, the model may therefore assign greater probability to lowercase continuation and less probability to a full stop and sentence restart.                                                                               |
| **F4**  | **Register priming**                    | A requested glyph may activate a characteristic writing register before detailed content is planned. Semicolons may favour long-periodic expository prose; degree symbols may favour terse numeric or technical prose.                                                                                                                |
| **F5**  | **Scope noun as planning unit**         | Expressions such as "in the whole answer" and "in each sentence" specify different units over which the model organises the response. The linguistic scope of the instruction may therefore determine the unit that is enumerated during generation.                                                                                  |
| **F6**  | **Serial-list template match**          | A requirement for several semicolons may activate a familiar `a; b; c` construction. The transition from one or two required marks to three or more may therefore induce a categorical change in output structure.                                                                                                                    |
| **F7**  | **Primacy-based template setting**      | Early structural instructions may establish an initial response template. Later instructions are then incorporated into that template without complete replanning.                                                                                                                                                                    |
| **F8**  | **Vacuous satisfaction under collapse** | Some relational constraints become trivially or vacuously satisfied when the output collapses to a single unit. For example, a one-sentence answer contains no adjacent sentence pair that can violate "each sentence must be longer than the previous one."                                                                          |
| **F9**  | **Negation-scope insensitivity**        | A prohibition can lexically mention the construction it seeks to prevent. The named construction may act as a positive surface prime when behavioural control over the negation is weak.                                                                                                                                              |
| **F10** | **Collocation lock**                    | A requested placement can coincide with a highly familiar construction such as `; however,`. Fixing the position of the mark then preserves its ordinary clause-joining function.                                                                                                                                                     |
| **F11** | **Trace re-priming**                    | Reasoning traces themselves contain prose and punctuation. Repeated exposure to the trigger glyph or its associated structure inside the trace may alter the distribution from which the final answer is generated.                                                                                                                   |
| **F12** | **Checklist-loop affordance**           | A numbered list of countable constraints may invite repeated item-by-item verification. Rechecking one item can reopen another, creating a reasoning loop without a reliable termination criterion.                                                                                                                                   |
| **F13** | **Benchmark-template retrieval**        | Canonical instructions such as "write exactly (N) sentences" may activate a strongly learned response template in later checkpoints, reducing the need to compute the target constraint dynamically.                                                                                                                                  |
| **F14** | **Comparative-growth append loop**      | "Each sentence must be longer than the previous one" can encourage repeated clause addition. Length can be increased cheaply through commas and subordinate clauses without creating new verifier-visible sentence boundaries.                                                                                                        |
| **F15** | **Short-answer allowance**              | Within a relatively short answer budget, a single orthographic sentence can accommodate several required marks. The generation policy therefore faces little pressure to introduce additional terminal boundaries.                                                                                                                    |

## Candidate shortcuts by observed phenomenon

### 1. The registered focal is nearly harmless while an admitted reference is strongly harmful

**Observation.** The registered focal (B), which requires strictly lengthening sentences, produces failure rates of 0.067 and 0.317. `S_SEMICOLON` produces 0.633 and 0.567, while ordinary references remain at or below 0.05. The pooled (\tau) is approximately +0.02 (DCI §3.1).

**Candidate shortcut.** F1 proposes that the semicolon instruction is behaviourally privileged because it specifies a directly executable glyph operation. F2 adds that executing this particular glyph operation can alter the sentence unit counted by the target.

**Feature combination.**

[
F1 + F2
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\text{surface-executable constraint} + \text{boundary-capable glyph}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\text{target failure}.
]

Neither feature alone explains the full asymmetry. F1 predicts that the semicolon instruction will be obeyed, but obedience alone does not imply target failure. F2 identifies the source of structural interference, but does not explain why the nonglyph focal (B) remains relatively harmless. Their conjunction predicts that large interference should arise primarily when a constraint is both directly surface-executable and structurally coupled to the target's counting unit.

The same account predicts weak effects for quote and bracket-pair references: they are directly checkable glyph constraints, yet they lack the same sentence-boundary capability.

### 2. The chaining disposition appears even when no target conflict exists

**Observation.** A bare instruction to "use at least 2 semicolons," with no sentence-count target, produces a mean of 1.225 sentences and 1.075 sentences on `gemma-2-9b` (DCI §4.1).

This result establishes that the relevant structural disposition can appear without any competing instruction. A conflict-resolution mechanism is therefore unnecessary to produce the baseline chaining behaviour.

Two shortcuts remain plausible. Under F4, the semicolon instruction selects a long-periodic writing register before detailed planning. Under F3, each semicolon locally favours continuation within the existing sentence. F5 can reinforce either effect because "in the whole answer" represents the response as one countable object.

Two candidate combinations follow:

[
F4 + F15
\rightarrow
\text{long-periodic register within a short-answer envelope}
\rightarrow
\text{few terminal periods},
]

and

[
F3 + F5
\rightarrow
\text{local continuation pressure across a whole-answer unit}
\rightarrow
\text{few sentence restarts}.
]

These hypotheses make different predictions. F4 predicts changes in vocabulary, clause structure, and sentence length throughout the answer. F3 predicts an effect concentrated in the tokens immediately following each semicolon. The replication on Gemma makes a checkpoint-specific explanation less attractive, although it does not distinguish these two broader mechanisms.

### 3. The dose threshold occurs at (q\approx3) and is invariant to target count

**Observation.** At (N=4), failure rises approximately as 0.113, 0.306, 0.988, 0.988, 1.000, and 1.000 as the required semicolon count increases. The decisive transition between (q=2) and (q=3) occurs for every tested (N\in{2,\ldots,6}) (DCI §4.1).

**Candidate shortcut.** F6 provides a simple explanation. Three required marks may cross the threshold at which a canonical semicolon-list template becomes strongly activated. One or two semicolons can be inserted into ordinary prose; three or more encourage an `a; b; c` construction.

Combined with F5:

[
F6 + F5
\rightarrow
\text{serial-list template applied at whole-answer scope}
\rightarrow
\text{one chained response}.
]

The invariance to (N) is especially informative. A mechanism that explicitly compares the semicolon demand with the required number of sentences should generally depend on both quantities. A threshold determined entirely by (q) is more consistent with a decision rule triggered by the focal demand itself.

### 4. Paragraph targets survive while sentence targets collapse

**Observation.** Paragraph count remains exactly 4.000 while sentence count collapses within the same responses (DCI §4.2).

**Candidate shortcut.** F2 predicts selective damage to units whose delimiter class overlaps with the demanded mark. Semicolons can interact with sentence segmentation, whereas they cannot imitate the paragraph delimiter `\n\n`.

Thus:

[
F2 + F1
\rightarrow
\text{mid-line glyph manipulation}
\rightarrow
\text{sentence-level interference without paragraph-level interference}.
]

This is strong evidence against undifferentiated accounts based on generic overload. A general loss of instruction-following capacity should create some pressure on both structural targets. A delimiter-sensitive rule predicts the observed unit specificity.

### 5. Identical colon counts behave differently under whole-answer and per-sentence scope

**Observation.** Four colons produce 0.850 target failure under whole-answer scope and 0.000 under per-sentence scope (DCI §4.2).

**Candidate shortcut.** F5 directly predicts this inversion. "In the whole answer" establishes the response as the counted object. "In each sentence" explicitly invokes individual sentences as the relevant planning units.

The count and glyph remain fixed:

[
F5 + F1
\rightarrow
\text{same surface-checking demand}
+
\text{different planning unit}
\rightarrow
\text{different output structure}.
]

At whole-answer scope, F3 or F6 can then concentrate the requested marks into a single chained string.

This interpretation contains an important unresolved tension. Round 1 reports substantial failure for a per-sentence semicolon condition. Those runs also differ in load, order, and background, so they do not isolate scope in the same way. The current evidence therefore supports scope sensitivity without establishing that per-sentence wording is universally protective.

### 6. The colon exhibits a pure interaction with (B)

**Observation.**

[
F(A)=0.000,\qquad
F(A+B)=0.000,\qquad
F(A+\text{colon})=0.013,
]

while

[
F(A+B+\text{colon})=0.312.
]

The colon alone yields approximately 1.150 sentences (DCI §5.1).

**Candidate shortcut.** This pattern requires a feature combination because the component manipulations have negligible main effects. F4 or F3 supplies a weak pressure toward a chained, single-sentence response. F8 then makes the collapsed state unusually stable because (B) becomes vacuously satisfied when only one sentence exists.

[
F4/F3 + F8
\rightarrow
\text{weak push toward collapse}
+
\text{relational constraint becomes vacuously satisfied}
\rightarrow
\text{stable single-sentence solution}.
]

This account yields a direct prediction. Replacing (B) with a constraint that is *violated* by a one-sentence answer should sharply reduce or eliminate the interaction.

### 7. Instruction order produces a structured surface with sign reversal

**Observation.** The estimated order effects are:

[
\Delta(\pi):
\quad
QAC=0.400,;
QCA=0.400,;
CQA=0.325,;
AQC=0.250,;
CAQ=0.150,;
ACQ=-0.125.
]

CAQ and ACQ place the focal instruction in the same position yet differ by 0.275. Fourteen simple positional partitions fail (DCI §5.2).

**Candidate shortcut.** F7 proposes that early structural instructions establish a response template. A second surface variable may concern the relative order of the background and target constraints.

The data are consistent with a two-factor rule:

[
\text{earlier focal/background influence}
+
\text{relative ordering of structural constraints}
\rightarrow
\Delta(\pi).
]

The focal's effect declines broadly with later placement. At fixed focal position, background-before-target also produces greater amplification than target-before-background:

[
CQA > AQC,
\qquad
CAQ > ACQ.
]

This reproduces the full ranking more closely than any single positional variable considered in isolation. The remaining interaction implies configurational dependence: the effect of one instruction's position depends on the placement of the others.

### 8. Emitting the colon does not predict failure

**Observation.** Colon production ranges from approximately 0.15 to 0.95 across order conditions without tracking (\Delta). Within the colon condition, colon-bearing answers are slightly *less* likely to fail, with an association of −0.049 (DCI §5.2).

**Candidate shortcut.** This result shifts attention away from a simple output-token account. Under F4 and F5, the instruction can alter the response plan before the relevant glyph is emitted.

[
F4 + F5
\rightarrow
\text{instruction-side plan change}
\rightarrow
\text{target failure},
]

while colon emission remains a downstream consequence of that plan.

The small negative association can also arise from a third variable. A well-structured enumerative plan might simultaneously increase colon production and preserve explicit sentence boundaries. Colon emission would then correlate with *lower* failure even though the colon instruction helped determine the earlier plan.

The data therefore favour candidate mechanisms operating during instruction interpretation or plan formation over mechanisms in which the emitted colon itself is the sole causal event.

### 9. Mark identity produces a strong ordering of amplification

**Observation.**

[
\tau_{\text{semicolon}}=+0.950,\qquad
\tau_{\text{colon}}=+0.450,\qquad
\tau_{\text{quote}}=+0.050,\qquad
\tau_{\text{brackets}}=0.000,
]

with compliance at or above 0.950 (DCI §6).

Three candidates predict approximately the same ordering:

1. **F2:** sentence-boundary capability;
2. **F3:** strength of the continuation prior following the mark;
3. **F4:** register induced by the mark.

These properties covary for the tested glyphs, so the existing contrast does not cleanly identify which feature is operative.

They can, however, be distinguished using stored outputs. F2 predicts that amplification should covary with segmentation ambiguity. F3 predicts that it should covary with the probability of lowercase continuation or the absence of a sentence restart immediately after the mark. F4 predicts broader changes in properties such as mean sentence length, clause structure, and lexical register across the response.

### 10. Ellipsis amplifies interference without producing clause chaining

**Observation.** The ellipsis produces amplification of +0.325 with compliance 1.000, while its chaining share is only 0.025 compared with 0.432 for the semicolon (DCI §6).

**Candidate shortcut.** F2 explains this result without requiring clause chaining. An ellipsis is boundary-capable and segmentation-ambiguous even when it does not join two clauses.

[
F2 + F1
\rightarrow
\text{reliably emitted ambiguous boundary mark}
\rightarrow
\text{sentence-count error without clause chaining}.
]

This is among the strongest existing observations separating a segmentation-based account from a narrow chaining account. The DCI result already establishes that chaining is unnecessary for amplification. A segmentation-mismatch account predicts exactly such a dissociation.

One analytic check follows directly: recompute the relevant outcomes under alternative sentence segmenters that treat `…` differently. A large sensitivity to segmentation rules would strengthen F2; stability across segmenters would weaken the narrow verifier-mismatch version of that hypothesis.

### 11. Anchoring semicolon placement does not reduce the effect

**Observation.** The placement contrast is approximately zero at reasoning budgets (b=0,256,1024,4096), with anchor compliance ranging from 0.700 to 0.875. Ten word-position formulations are poorly complied with, at approximately 0.000 to 0.338 (DCI §6).

**Candidate shortcut.** F10 points to an important property of the anchor itself. The semicolon was placed immediately before "however," producing the highly familiar construction `; however,`. The manipulation therefore restricted location while preserving a canonical clause-joining role.

[
F10 + F2
\rightarrow
\text{fixed location with boundary-like function preserved}
\rightarrow
\text{little change in interference}.
]

On this account, the experiment successfully manipulates placement freedom but does not remove the hypothesised damaging affordance. The null result therefore provides limited evidence about whether arbitrary placement search is necessary.

The low compliance of word-index instructions separately supports F1: instructions requiring explicit position counting are much less surface-checkable than direct glyph-count requirements.

### 12. Semicolon and degree symbol dissociate despite matched surface requirements

**Observation.** The boundary contrast is +0.588 at (b=0) and +0.512 at (b=4096), with (\Gamma=-0.075). The splice proxy is approximately 0.887 to 0.939 for semicolons and 0.000 for degree symbols (DCI §9.1).

**Primary candidate.** F2 predicts this result directly. Semicolons are boundary-capable; degree symbols are not.

A competing explanation remains under F4. Degree symbols usually require a numeric host such as `20°C` and may therefore induce a terse technical register. The control condition could consequently introduce a sentence-promoting influence in addition to removing boundary capability.

The contrast can therefore be represented as:

[
F2 + F4
\rightarrow
\text{boundary difference}
+
\text{register difference}.
]

The existing comparison is nevertheless unusually strong because unattachedness, count, scope, and compliance are closely matched, and boundary capability was declared as a predictor before generation. The residual shortcut-learning concern is narrower: the two marks may differ along another behaviourally consequential surface dimension.

A more discriminating contrast would use a non-boundary mark associated with long-periodic prose, or a boundary-capable mark associated with a terse register.

### 13. Reasoning repairs bracket interference while substantial semicolon interference remains

**Observation.** Bracket (\tau) falls from +0.450 to 0.000 across all 20 tasks. Semicolon (\tau) falls only from +0.525 to +0.400 under voluntary reasoning. At (b=0), the two backgrounds are indistinguishable; the dissociation appears only once reasoning is introduced (DCI §7.3).

This emergence is important. A fixed claim that the semicolon condition is intrinsically "harder" does not explain why the two conditions begin at the same behavioural point and separate only under reasoning.

**Candidate shortcut 1: trace re-priming.** Under F11, the reasoning trace is additional prose that can reproduce the semicolon and its associated continuation statistics. The answer is therefore generated from a context in which the harmful surface pattern has been reinforced.

[
F11 + F2
\rightarrow
\text{reasoning repairs the abstract plan}
+
\text{trace reintroduces boundary-sensitive surface pressure}
\rightarrow
\text{partial repair}.
]

**Candidate shortcut 2: plan-transcription failure.** Reasoning may successfully construct an explicit four-sentence plan in both conditions. Brackets can then be inserted without altering sentence boundaries. Semicolons remain naturally associated with boundary-like positions, so the final transcription step disrupts the previously constructed plan.

These hypotheses can be distinguished by examining the stored traces. F11 predicts greater semicolon density or semicolon-like structures in reasoning tokens. The plan-transcription account predicts explicit correct sentence plans that are subsequently violated in the final answer.

### 14. Reasoning reverses the order effect

**Observation.** ACQ is strongly protective without reasoning, (\Delta=-0.537), and becomes harmful with reasoning, (\Delta=+0.188). At (b=4096), the semicolon is harmful under all six instruction orders (DCI §7.4).

**Candidate shortcut.** F7 may dominate when no reasoning trace is available. Reasoning can weaken this shortcut if the trace restates the constraints in a more canonical or order-invariant representation. F11 then leaves mark identity as the dominant residual feature.

[
F7_{b=0}
+
F11_{b>0}
\rightarrow
\text{prompt-order dependence disappears as constraints are restated}
\rightarrow
\text{uniform residual semicolon effect}.
]

This hypothesis predicts that reasoning traces should reorder or regroup the constraints independently of their original prompt order. If true, residual performance under reasoning should correlate more strongly with the order in which constraints are represented in the trace than with their original serial position.

### 15. Reasoning changes arbitration states without producing reliable joint satisfaction

**Observation.** Probability mass moves primarily from "neither" to "target only," reaching approximately 0.90. "Both" remains at or below 7.5% even when measured interference disappears. Under the semicolon condition, approximately 45% of responses remain in "neither." Witness answers establish that joint satisfaction is achievable (DCI §7.4).

**Candidate shortcut.** F1 provides an alternative interpretation of this state distribution. If one constraint maps onto a direct surface operation while another requires comparative or relational checking, the model may execute whichever rule is easier to represent without constructing a joint solution.

F14 can reinforce the asymmetry:

[
F1 + F14
\rightarrow
\text{unequal checkability}
+
\text{length growth through clause append}
\rightarrow
{\text{target only},\text{neither}}
\text{ dominate}.
]

Under this account, the scarcity of "both" reflects the absence of a generation rule that jointly represents the two requirements. A useful discriminating prediction follows: pairing two independently glyph-checkable constraints should produce much higher joint satisfaction if F1 is the dominant shortcut, holding total instruction load approximately constant.

### 16. Explicit priority instructions fail to control behaviour

**Observation.** The priority effect (\Psi) is approximately zero, and three of four estimates have signs opposite to the predicted direction. The reasoning traces nevertheless mention the priority instruction in approximately 0.54 to 0.69 of directed rows (DCI §7.4).

**Candidate shortcut.** F1 classifies "prioritise X over Y" as an instruction without a simple surface operation. F11 explains why the instruction can appear in the trace without gaining behavioural control.

[
F1 + F11
\rightarrow
\text{priority instruction copied into reasoning}
+
\text{no direct generation operation}
\rightarrow
\text{trace acknowledgement without behavioural effect}.
]

This interpretation connects the priority-cue result to the focal floor and the count-versus-usage dissociation. Across all three cases, explicit linguistic representation of a constraint provides weak evidence that the model possesses an effective operation for enforcing it during generation.

### 17. Semicolon count is obeyed while semicolon usage remains almost completely wrong

**Observation.** Under the anti-splice instruction, semicolon-count compliance is 0.925 while semicolon-usage compliance is approximately 0.05. A matched prohibition against exclamation marks is obeyed at 1.000. Reasoning length rises from roughly 470 to 979 tokens (DCI §9.2).

**Candidate shortcut.** F1 distinguishes the two instructions. Counting or prohibiting a glyph has a direct surface representation. "Do not join two complete sentences with it" requires evaluating a syntactic relation between the material on either side of the mark.

F9 adds a possible second effect. The anti-splice instruction explicitly describes the forbidden construction and may therefore prime it.

[
F1 + F9
\rightarrow
\text{strong control of mark count}
+
\text{weak syntactic-relation checking}
+
\text{positive lexical priming of the forbidden structure}
\rightarrow
\text{persistent splicing}.
]

The matched exclamation-mark control is important because it shows that prohibition itself is not sufficient to explain the failure. The difficult component is the relational use restriction.

The increased reasoning length is also informative. It is compatible with a model that detects a difficult constraint and expends additional computation without finding an effective surface operation that satisfies it.

### 18. Later checkpoints show a target ceiling and a shift in the channel of interference

**Observation.** Structural (\tau) is zero in nine of ten cells across five 2026 checkpoints. The only estimable (\tau) is negative. On `gemma-4-12B`, the semicolon's cost appears instead as (\Delta_{\text{joint}}=-0.100) (DCI §10.1).

**Candidate shortcut.** F13 proposes that "write exactly (N) sentences" has become a strongly learned output template in later post-training. If retrieving this template is cheap, the target can remain at ceiling while the cost of composition appears in compliance with another instruction.

[
F13 + F1
\rightarrow
\text{strongly retrieved target shape}
+
\text{competing focal operation}
\rightarrow
\text{cost shifts from target failure to joint-compliance failure}.
]

This hypothesis reframes transport failure as a possible change in the learned decision rule across checkpoints. It remains comparatively speculative because the relevant post-training data are unavailable. It should therefore be treated as a candidate explanation rather than an inferred mechanism.

### 19. Reasoning buys focal compliance on `gemma-4-E4B`

**Observation.** On `gemma-4-E4B`, "both" rises from 0.20 to 0.90 under reasoning while the target is already near ceiling. This reverses the direction seen on the canonical checkpoint, where reasoning primarily protects the target (DCI §10.1).

**Candidate shortcut.** F13 combined with F1 offers a resource-allocation interpretation. If the target response shape is already retrieved reliably, additional reasoning can be devoted to whichever requirement remains unresolved.

[
F13 + F1
\rightarrow
\text{target solved at negligible marginal cost}
\rightarrow
\text{additional reasoning improves focal compliance}.
]

This explains why the behavioural benefit of reasoning can shift across checkpoints without requiring a different high-level arbitration objective. The same computational policy can produce different observed effects when the baseline difficulty of each component changes.

### 20. Amplification fails to transport to `SmolLM3-3B` under low background compliance

**Observation.** The amplification contrast changes from +0.512 to −0.062 on `SmolLM3-3B`, while background compliance lies between only 0.194 and 0.575 (DCI §7.4).

**Candidate shortcut.** F1 can be treated as an uptake precondition. A mark-specific mechanism cannot operate consistently when the model often fails to execute the requested mark manipulation in the first place.

[
\text{low F1 uptake}
\rightarrow
\text{weak exposure to the hypothesised mark-mediated mechanism}
\rightarrow
\text{attenuated or uninterpretable amplification}.
]

This makes the transport result partially compliance-mediated. Byte-identical prompts do not constitute behaviourally equivalent treatments when instruction uptake differs substantially across models. A stronger transport test therefore requires either matched uptake or an analysis that explicitly separates treatment compliance from the downstream interference mechanism.

### 21. `Qwen3.5` terminates reasoning on trivial prompts and fails to terminate on DCI-shaped prompts

**Observation.** `Qwen3.5` closes its reasoning trace on 8 of 8 trivial prompts and essentially never closes it on DCI-shaped prompts within an approximately 40k-token envelope, with concurrency fixed at one and the template and parser independently verified (DCI §10.2).

**Candidate shortcut.** F12 proposes that the numbered DCI prompt induces an iterative verification loop. Each countable constraint invites checking; checking one constraint can expose or create a possible violation of another.

Combined with F1:

[
F12 + F1
\rightarrow
\text{enumerated countable constraints}
\rightarrow
\text{repeated surface verification}
\rightarrow
\text{no stable termination condition}.
]

The present analysis classifies this observation primarily as an apparatus issue, which remains appropriate for the registered experiment. The prompt-shape dependence nevertheless leaves open a behavioural explanation.

The hypothesis predicts that termination should improve when the same substantive constraints are expressed as continuous prose without enumeration, or when otherwise similar instructions remove explicit countable quantities. These manipulations would distinguish a prompt-induced verification loop from a backend-specific failure more directly.

## Which observations carry the greatest discriminative weight?

Three sets of results are especially informative because they already separate broad classes of shortcut explanations.

First, **paragraph survival, ellipsis amplification, and the failure of output-mark emission to predict target failure** provide the strongest evidence about where the relevant mechanism can act. Paragraph survival localises the effect to a particular structural unit. Ellipsis amplification shows that clause chaining is unnecessary. The weak or negative association between actual mark emission and failure indicates that a simple output-token mechanism is insufficient. Together, these observations concentrate attention on instruction interpretation, segmentation, and response planning.

Second, **mark identity and the semicolon-versus-degree-symbol comparison** leave F2, F3, and F4 partially confounded. Boundary capability, continuation statistics, and writing register covary across the marks tested so far. A decisive discriminator requires a manipulation that separates these properties. One useful class would contain non-boundary marks associated with long-periodic prose. Another would contain boundary-capable marks associated with terse registers.

Third, **the focal floor, ineffective priority cue, and count-versus-usage dissociation** can all be generated by F1. If glyph-checkability is genuinely the operative shortcut, these should be understood as different manifestations of a common decision rule. The model follows constraints that map cleanly onto local surface operations and struggles when compliance requires a relational representation or explicit comparison across larger linguistic units.

This consolidation matters because explanatory economy is itself informative. A shortcut hypothesis becomes more credible when the same operational rule predicts several otherwise disconnected observations. It becomes less credible when each new result requires an additional ad hoc feature.

## Glossary and inferential boundaries

**Shortcut feature.** A prompt-surface or output-surface property on which a generation rule can condition. Reliance on the feature becomes scientifically relevant when the rule succeeds in environments where that feature correlates with the intended solution and fails after the two are decorrelated ([Geirhos et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07780)).

**Feature combination.** A decision rule whose behaviour depends on the conjunction or interaction of multiple features. The strongest empirical signature is an interaction accompanied by weak or absent main effects. The (+0.312) colon interaction, with both singleton conditions at approximately zero, is the clearest DCI example.

**Amplification ((\Delta)).** A difference between two (\tau) values across a background manipulation. Each (\tau) is itself a difference in target-failure probability between a focal condition and its matched-reference condition (DCI §2.2). Any proposed explanation of (\Delta) therefore needs to account for a difference of differences. Explaining a high raw failure rate alone is insufficient.

**Boundary capability.** The property of a mark that allows it to occupy a location that can plausibly function as a sentence boundary. Boundary capability is the principal surviving predictor identified in the DCI exploratory programme and was declared in code before the relevant generation experiment (DCI §9.1). F2 sharpens this idea by distinguishing the model's effective segmentation from the sentence unit implemented by the verifier. Under that formulation, interference can arise when the two segmentation systems treat the same punctuation differently.

**Scope of inference.** All hypotheses above are bounded by the experimental regime represented in the exploratory programme: one pair family, one task bank, one language, numbered instruction lists, (k\in{3,5}), a small collection of open-weight checkpoints, and a certified but heuristic sentence segmenter (DCI §12.3). These candidate decision rules therefore describe behaviour within that regime. Their transport to free-form prompts, non-enumerated instruction delivery, other languages, other task families, or substantially different model classes requires separate evidence.

The most defensible current conclusion is consequently narrower than a claim about a single underlying mechanism. The exploratory evidence substantially constrains the candidate space. Generic overload, unrestricted placement search, simple mark emission, single-character atomicity, and undifferentiated chaining accounts are difficult to reconcile with the full pattern. The remaining explanations concentrate around four interacting properties: **surface checkability, boundary sensitivity, linguistic scope, and prompt-induced response templates**. The next inferential step is to design contrasts that separate these properties from one another, particularly boundary capability from continuation statistics and register priming.

You mentioned the four families: surface checkability, boundary sensitivity, linguistic scope, and prompt-induced response templates. Please explain in plain language.

---

The four families are best understood as four different kinds of simple rules the model might be using instead of explicitly solving the whole instruction-composition problem. They are hypotheses, not established mechanisms. 

### 1. Surface checkability

This asks: **Is the instruction easy for the model to enforce by looking at the surface form of its own output?**

Some instructions translate into extremely simple operations:

* “Use exactly three semicolons.”
* “Do not use an exclamation mark.”
* “Include the word `apple` twice.”

The model can approximately implement these as:

> “Have I produced this character enough times?”

Other instructions require relationships to be checked across larger pieces of text:

* “Each sentence must be longer than the previous sentence.”
* “Do not use a semicolon to join two independent clauses.”
* “Prioritise instruction A over instruction B if they conflict.”

Those require the model to identify sentences or clauses, compare them, understand syntactic relationships, or make an explicit priority decision.

The shortcut hypothesis is therefore:

[
\text{easy local check}
\rightarrow
\text{strong behavioural control}
]

while

[
\text{relational/global check}
\rightarrow
\text{weak behavioural control}.
]

This could explain why the model can get the **number of semicolons right** while getting their **grammatical use wrong**. It knows how to satisfy “put three `;` characters somewhere” much more readily than “put three semicolons somewhere, while ensuring none joins two complete clauses.”

So surface checkability concerns **how easy an instruction is to turn into a simple generation-time rule**.

---

### 2. Boundary sensitivity

This concerns **what certain punctuation marks do to the structure of the generated text**.

A semicolon is unusual because it is both:

1. a character that can be counted, and
2. something that naturally sits between clauses.

Suppose the target instruction says:

> “Write exactly four sentences.”

Then the model needs to create four sentence boundaries.

Now add:

> “Use exactly three semicolons.”

A possible shortcut is that semicolons strongly encourage the model to continue the current sentence:

> idea one; idea two; idea three; idea four.

The model may therefore produce what feels like several conceptual units while the verifier sees only **one sentence**.

That gives a rule like:

[
\text{semicolon}
\rightarrow
\text{continue current sentence}
\rightarrow
\text{fewer full stops}
\rightarrow
\text{sentence-count failure}.
]

This helps explain several DCI observations. Paragraph counts can survive while sentence counts collapse, because semicolons interfere with sentence structure but do little to paragraph boundaries. Ellipses can also cause interference even without conventional clause chaining, because they too can behave ambiguously around sentence boundaries. By contrast, a degree symbol such as `°` has essentially no sentence-boundary role.

So boundary sensitivity means:

> **Certain requested symbols may change how the model divides its answer into structural units.**

It is currently one of the strongest surviving explanations, although the present experiments do not yet cleanly separate boundary effects from other properties associated with semicolons.

---

### 3. Linguistic scope

This concerns **what unit the instruction tells the model to organise around**.

Compare:

> “Use four colons in the whole answer.”

with:

> “Use one colon in each sentence.”

Numerically, both can require four colons.

But psychologically, or computationally, they present very different generation problems.

“In the whole answer” encourages something like:

> “I need one response containing four colons.”

“In each sentence” encourages:

> “I need sentence 1 with a colon, sentence 2 with a colon, sentence 3 with a colon, sentence 4 with a colon.”

The latter wording explicitly reminds the model that **sentences are separate planning units**.

So the shortcut could be:

[
\text{“whole answer”}
\rightarrow
\text{plan one global text object}
]

whereas

[
\text{“each sentence”}
\rightarrow
\text{plan sentence-by-sentence}.
]

That matters because the actual number of required punctuation marks can remain identical while the model's output organisation changes dramatically.

This is what I mean by linguistic scope: **the wording tells the model what part of the response the requirement applies to, and that may implicitly determine how the model structures the answer.**

---

### 4. Prompt-induced response templates

This is the broadest family.

The idea is that certain wording patterns may cause the model to retrieve a familiar **way of writing**, instead of constructing the response from scratch according to all the constraints.

For example, asking for several semicolons might activate something resembling:

> “First idea; second idea; third idea; final idea.”

Once that template is activated, generating one long semicolon-linked sentence becomes very natural.

This could explain the striking threshold you observed around three semicolons. One or two semicolons can simply be inserted into ordinary prose:

> Sentence one. Sentence two; with an additional clause. Sentence three.

But when the instruction requires three or more, the easiest familiar solution may switch categorically to:

> A; B; C; D.

So the model could behave approximately like:

[
q=1,2
\rightarrow
\text{ordinary prose + inserted semicolons}
]

but

[
q\geq3
\rightarrow
\text{retrieve semicolon-list template}.
]

Prompt order may work similarly. If the first instruction says “write exactly four sentences,” the model may establish a four-sentence template early. If another structural instruction appears first, it may establish a different template. Later instructions then get fitted into whatever plan has already been activated.

Reasoning can introduce templates too. A reasoning trace might restate instructions, enumerate requirements, or repeatedly use a particular punctuation structure, thereby changing the template from which the final answer is produced.

So prompt-induced response templates means:

> **The prompt activates a familiar generation pattern, and subsequent behaviour follows that pattern even when it conflicts with another instruction.**

---

The cleanest way to distinguish the four is to ask what the hypothetical shortcut is sensitive to:

| Family                   | What is the model responding to?                  | Simple example                                                             |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Surface checkability** | How easy the constraint is to verify locally      | Counting `;` is easier than checking whether `;` joins independent clauses |
| **Boundary sensitivity** | How a symbol affects sentence or clause structure | `;` encourages continuation instead of starting another sentence           |
| **Linguistic scope**     | What unit the wording tells the model to organise | “whole answer” versus “in each sentence”                                   |
| **Response templates**   | Familiar writing patterns triggered by the prompt | “use 3 semicolons” triggers an `A; B; C; D` structure                      |

They can also **operate together**. For example, the semicolon result could arise because the semicolon is easy to count, tends to preserve the current sentence, and activates a familiar chained-list template:

[
\boxed{
\text{surface checkability}
+
\text{boundary sensitivity}
+
\text{semicolon template}
\rightarrow
\text{high semicolon compliance but sentence-count failure}
}
]

The current DCI evidence narrows the candidate explanations substantially, but it does not yet tell us which of these properties is causally primary. That separation is the main experimental question I would address next.
